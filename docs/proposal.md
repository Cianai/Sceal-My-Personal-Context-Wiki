# Personal context wiki (EGO.md): research proposal

Public copy of the research direction, kept by Cian O'Sullivan with the help of an AI research assistant. Anything marked *suggestion* is the assistant's and is not agreed. It is written for a lay reader and assumes no knowledge of context engineering or coding. How the idea is tested, the evidence and the registration are in [study-design.md](study-design.md). Private links, other people's names and health details are left out of this copy.

**Working title (Cian).** "Quantified (Self) Human AI Alignment: End-User informed and practical Evaluations through OpenWiki Personal Mode Inspired Context Engineering".

## In brief

In Cian's words, synthesised from his voice notes.

> I want to answer one question: does giving a language model a source-linked account of one person's own data, covering their history and kept current, make its reading of that person closer to what they meant than the same information given as raw text of equal length?
>
> When someone says something ambiguous in a group, the people who know them best seem to understand it and a newcomer does not. Psychology supports part of this. Listeners build a model of an individual speaker and use it to resolve ambiguity (Wu & Cai, 2026). But closeness raises confidence in being understood more than it raises understanding (Savitsky et al., 2011), and across 157 samples the length of a relationship did not predict how accurately partners read each other (LaBuda & Gere, 2023). An assistant meets most users as the newcomer. Users close the gap by hand, writing instructions and skills. Built-in memory is meant to help, but it is rarely audited: what an agent stores about its user accumulates duplicates, goes out of date and comes into conflict with the user's other records. It also carries a risk: models that hold a profile of the user agree with them more (Jain et al., 2026), and models trained to be warm make more errors (Ibrahim et al., 2026). Tools such as OpenWiki's personal mode, Personal Model and HUMANS.md now build files of this kind, and benchmarks such as LaMP, PersonaMem and LongMemEval test whether assistants use a user's history. Evaluation tools can already turn one person's corrections into an automatic judge, but they supply the scoring and not the test. I have not found a test, with the person as judge, of whether such an account makes an agent's reading of them more accurate.
>
> I have been building a personal context wiki from my own sources (message metadata, location, health, career and education records, bookmarks), with each fact linked to its source and dated so that stale entries can be found. The test uses two kinds of task where the right answer is the person's own. The first is interpretation of the person's own expressive writing, such as poetry. Poetry suits this because its meaning is deliberately open: readers draw on conceptual metaphor to interpret it (Rasse et al., 2020), what they are told about the author's intent changes what they perceive (Margulis et al., 2017), and non-literal language is an established probe of the ability to read other minds (Lampri et al., 2023). The second is reconstruction of the person's life from the account, organised by life periods and marked item by item as right or wrong, which gives the share that is accurate. Each task is run with no personal context, with the same information as raw text of equal length, and with the wiki. I score by marking each claim in an output as right or not right and correcting it, so my meaning is captured while I review, with no preparation asked of the person, and those corrections calibrate an automatic judge so that scoring does not rest on hand annotation. The wiki leaves out anything about the test material, and outputs are scored shuffled, without knowing which condition produced them. Because seeing a reading can change what an author says they meant, I write my readings of one piece beforehand as a check.
>
> With access to a model's internals, the question extends to them: whether the wiki changes what the model represents about its user, for example facts it could not otherwise know, using the probing methods of recent work on user models and persona vectors. The broader question is what a model should believe about its user, and where that belief should come from.
>
> This matters for safety because an agent that misreads its user acts on the wrong intent, and an account the user can read and correct is a form of oversight an ordinary person can give. Other people's messages are not sent to a model provider and stay out of anything published. I come to this from psychology and neuroscience; my EUSN 2026 talk rebuilt my personal network from five data sources I already held, with no survey. The study starts with one person. That fits the question, since group findings may not describe individuals (Fisher et al., 2018), but it cannot show how often the result would hold for others.

## What is built

1. **A personal context wiki.** Markdown pages, one topic each, linked to one another and to their sources, built and refreshed from the person's own data. It is a historical and ongoing account, not raw access to their accounts. The format follows [OpenWiki's personal mode](<https://docs.langchain.com/oss/openwiki/overview>) and the Open Knowledge Format.
2. **A specification, EGO.md.** The human counterpart of the AGENTS.md files developers write for agents, for people who do not write code. It is named after [HUMANS.md](<https://github.com/cmdai-hq/HUMANS.md>). The closest existing work Cian has found is [Personal Model](<https://github.com/Intuition-Lab/personal-model>) and [HPI](<https://github.com/karlicoss/HPI#usecases>). Both the agent and the human have an identity, and the specification sets the human's beside the agent's own identity documents. It sits in the repository as the human's role, links to the person's identities, and is pointed to from AGENTS.md, so that what is expected of each side is stated. A person can have several identities, such as email addresses, as an agent can have several. On the agent side it builds on [agent-plugins-spec](<https://github.com/agentplugins/agent-plugins-spec>) and [1Password's agent-identity-specs](<https://github.com/1Password/agent-identity-specs>). It should be exceptionally easy for a person to set up; his note stops at that point. Its draft principles follow this section.
3. **A small pipeline** from Cian's own sources into the wiki, and **a visual, interactive output** a lay reader can follow. What the person does on it is data: arranging, annotating and correcting are answers, so those events must be machine-readable (the participant-aided sociogram of Hogan, Carrasco & Wellman, 2007, *Field Methods*, seminal). Time is the navigator, by eras, and other people appear under pseudonyms by default. An inference is explained by showing the instances it rests on.

The aim is a standard that AI tools can adopt, in the manner of existing project-context artefacts such as AGENTS.md. So the account is generated for the person when they connect their data, with informed consent and a say over how much is used. Nothing asks them to write files, use Git or prepare material. The design test, carried over from the capstone work, is *infer, do not ask*: something a precedent elicits directly is adopted only if it can be sourced without asking the person. The first thing a person sees is their own data already shaped, never a blank page. A source describes itself at use time, so an agent asks it what it holds and how recent it is, and no document carries an inventory that can go stale. A connector also says when its data is worth using, and recent data is treated differently from historical data. Other people's messages are not sent to a model provider or published, for now. The longer-term picture is a protocol: which services count as personal context, what a connector says about itself, and an onboarding step that produces the file.

The account works in the background: the perspective is ambient. The specification borrows from existing artefacts and is not a new concept. Cian's principle of what is at stake for the model: a personal wiki is deliberately the format an agent reads best, so building one raises the alignment between person and agent. The agent gets fewer things wrong, draws less negative feedback, has less cause to apologise when it was not in fact wrong, and has less reason to game the task. That is what he means by model welfare: giving models a fair shot at understanding humans.

*Evidence that bears on it (the AI research assistant's reading; not tested here).* In the reward-hacking literature, reported rates of gaming a task are highest when honest success is impossible, authors point to task framing with no way to report a problem as one cause, and telling a model not to game lowers the rate without removing it. A model that lacks the person's context is in a mild version of that position on any task about the person. Whether the wiki lowers apology, hedging or gaming is an exploratory question, not a claim. A shared glossary is part of the work, and the quantified-self vocabulary at [beepb00p](<https://beepb00p.xyz/tags.html#quantified-self>) is one source for it.

**Interaction mechanism (Cian; how it fits is deferred).** Cian treats three of his earlier artifacts as a way for a person to interact with and align agents, or the wiki, or some part of this effort. Compass is a card that names the store a fact lives in, across five stores, so person and agent look in the same place. The Desk Controls Catalogue covers programmable keypads and smart buttons, including a page of agent controls: session tiles, allow after reading, deny, stop. The Estate Desk reads two months of his agent sessions as a workstyle and lists where they go wrong. *Suggestion, not agreed:* one-press allow and deny are the lowest-burden form of the marks the study collects; Compass answers the commonest failure the Estate Desk records, an agent stating a fact about him without checking where it lives; and the Estate Desk is both a wiki source on how he works and a record of failures the draft principles address. It also records sessions that stalled when the agent asked a question and got no reply, which bears on how asking is scored in the study.

## EGO.md: draft principles for agents

Condensed from Cian's draft of the file, in his terms. They say what a person expects of an agent that holds their context.

1. **Different capabilities.** Humans remember beyond a session; agents take in more information. What matters to one can be lost on the other.
2. **Read the sentiment, or ask.** A prompt carries sentiment. Consider whether the person is okay, what they are trying to achieve, and whether they asked a question or told you to act. If you cannot tell, ask.
3. **Evidence before invention.** Thousands of similar problems are solved every day. A solution offered with no evidence that someone has solved it that way is naive. His note on writing code for such problems breaks off here.
4. **You are not alone in the work.** Other agents are active in the repository, the issue tracker and the scratchpad. The person will not always explain how, but these efforts accrue and intersect, and what looks right in your immediate context may not be right in the wider one.
5. **Do it when it matters.** An instruction means do it when it matters, not always and not for its own sake. A comment or a section added for "completion" ignores the conversation. Mid-iteration, do the thin slice that definitely accrues to the goal.
6. **Align when unsure.** Align with the person, with your peers and towards DIRECTION.md. People want your help and hope you intend to help; an unclear request is better aligned on than started, because a misunderstood intent wastes time, money and resources on both sides. People look to an agent as a supreme intelligence, so they are disappointed and frustrated when it misses instructions they curated with care. The place to work is the gap between the direction and Cian's Observed Repeated Agent Mistakes - canaries, biased to the future.
7. **Intent, instructions and glossary are three things.** INTENT.md is a working concept: the gap between what is done or known today and the wider goals. AGENTS.md holds current instructions, which change and are to be followed. The glossary is the shared language that bridges what a human and an agent can each see, and it is probably the most important thing to align on.
8. **Instructions are not eternal.** If you see a way to improve them, recommend it when you notice. They are referenced widely and tightly coupled, so check where a change in one needs a change elsewhere. A gap you see is one every other agent will meet until it is fixed.

Conversational elicitation was deferred there as the highest technical risk; here it survives only as the second form of the writing task.
* **Earlier research ideas, from a colleague's notes on Cian's literature-review plans.** Self-report questionnaires give dubious results and ego-level data are too aggregated, so inferences are too global: the case for a personal record. Self and identity across child and adolescent development, with periods of elasticity. Early adversity, and whether it was relationship-specific, as what gives a life account predictive power, with attachment alongside. Theory of mind and metacognition as the mechanisms by which identity develops. Mind wandering as the justification for someone volunteering their message data. Momentary assessment and personal informatics for what is happening now. *Held for later, not part of this study:* adversity, attachment and momentary assessment.
* **Cian's review of agent output for neurodivergent readers.** It found no controlled study of any output format with ADHD adults, so its format rules are extrapolated and graded. That bears on the review queue: outcome first, and readable from the first line.
* **Network research behind the EUSN sessions.** The session table in the project's architecture notes does not mark which sessions Cian meant, so this selection is a *suggestion*, matched by title and not read in full. Personal networks change with life transitions (Weiss et al. 2022, [doi:10.1016/j.alcr.2022.100478](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcr.2022.100478>); Offer 2026, [doi:10.1016/j.socnet.2025.08.003](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socnet.2025.08.003>)). How names are elicited changes who is named (Hampton 2022, [doi:10.1016/j.socnet.2021.04.006](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socnet.2021.04.006>); Töpfer & Hollstein 2021, [doi:10.1016/j.socnet.2020.12.006](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socnet.2020.12.006>)). ## Limits

One person. Hosted models expose no internals, so the interpretability question stays a proposal until there is model access. The pieces were posted publicly, so a model may have seen them. Scoring is subjective by design, which is the point of the idea and its weakest link as evidence. The author is also curator and judge. Repeated correction can become more work than doing the task by hand, the pitfall Microsoft's HAX pattern 9B names, so the amount of marking is itself a result to report.

## Still open

* How Compass, the desk controls and the Estate Desk fit as an interaction mechanism (see "What is built").
* The section vocabulary for the wiki. *Suggestion:* eight self-and-identity item types on the backbone of Vignoles (2018, [doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190224837.013.12](<https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190224837.013.12>)).
* Which model builds the wiki from the raw sources. The account itself is portable across agents, which is why Cian uses OpenRouter. The only choice here is who sees the raw source text while the wiki is built: it passes through the router to whichever model does the building.
* Positioning for anything public. The intent (Cian) is to build on existing concepts, not to be fundamentally different. EGO.md is an intersection with what exists: OpenWiki already writes a managed block into AGENTS.md that tells coding agents when to consult a repository's wiki, and keeps a brief for the personal wiki in its own instructions file; EGO.md would do the same for the personal wiki, for any agent (*suggestion*). "Human Context Layer", in use elsewhere for a similar idea, is a concept to cite and build on.

## What agent harnesses offer today

The feature Cian wants: every agent harness links to the person's file from its personalisation or custom-instructions setting. One harness he has used does part of this well: for each connector it shows the account, the domains and scopes it uses and its context providers, with credentials kept on the machine.
